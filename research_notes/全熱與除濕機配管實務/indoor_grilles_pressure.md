## Q1. Typical placement rules: which rooms get supply vs extract, supply–return spacing in the same room, mounting height, keeping jets off people, and sidewall throw for a ~7 m room

### Takeaway
The authoritative pattern is zoning: supply (送風) to "clean" living rooms and bedrooms, extract (回風/排風) from wet/dirty rooms or a central corridor, with air crossing each room toward its exit path (door/transfer opening). No manufacturer or standard found gives a numeric minimum indoor supply–return distance; the rule is "keep them apart, on opposite ends/diagonals, and don't put the supply next to the room's exit path." A high sidewall grille for a 7 m throw should sit close to the ceiling (catalog throws are tested about 200–230 mm below the ceiling, using the Coanda effect) and be chosen from the catalog throw table at the real airflow, remembering that non-isothermal air changes throw by about ±30%.

### Cited Findings
**Zoning (supply / transfer / extract)**
- A Passive House design text divides the dwelling into a supply-air zone (living rooms, bedrooms), an extract zone (kitchens, bathrooms, WCs) and transfer zones in between (corridors); air moves "through transfer paths, such as gaps beneath doors, at the door head or through overflow valves." — [Detail, Passive House Design (issuu)](https://issuu.com/detail-magazine/docs/bk_passive_house_design/12)
- Cascade ventilation (Pfluger, Univ. Innsbruck / PHI-associated project) routes air from supply rooms through an overflow zone such as a corridor to the exhaust rooms; "compared to a system with supply and extract air for each room, this principle already saves a lot of space and money." Small "active overflow" fans can push corridor air into bedrooms/living rooms, which is useful in retrofits. — [Pfluger, SINFONIA / passiv.de](https://sinfonia.passiv.de/?q=node/130)
- A 2012 AIVC/TightVent paper found that the pressure drop across overflow elements affects ventilation heat losses, so transfer-path design has an energy cost. — [AIVC 2012](https://www.aivc.org/sites/default/files/19%20AIVC_TIGHTVENT_Conference_Paper_2012.pdf)
- Delta Electronics (Taiwan ERV maker) FAQ: put the supply outlet (送風口) in the occupied activity area and the return (回風口) where air is dirtier and poorly ventilated; "送風口應與回風口保持一段距離，以防止氣流短路現象發生" (keep supply and return some distance apart to prevent short-circuiting). No distance in metres is given. — [Delta FAQ 2020](https://www.deltaww.com/zh-tw/FAQ/2020)
- The same Delta FAQ: outdoor fresh-air intake and exhaust terminals must be ≥1.5 m apart (outdoor short-circuit), and the ERV main unit should not be installed in hot/humid places such as kitchens or toilets (unit service life). — [Delta FAQ 2020](https://www.deltaww.com/zh-tw/FAQ/2020)

**Short-circuit / supply vs return spacing**
- A SHASE (Japan) paper reports that "if the layout of supply openings and exhaust ones is not appropriate, the short circuit between them appears" and ventilation/air-conditioning efficiency drops sharply. — [SHASE Transactions](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/shase/32/126/32_KJ00006793186/_article/-char/en)
- German building-services guidance: avoid indoor supply/exhaust short-circuits by separating the openings far enough, or by using long-throw nozzles. — [baukobox](https://baukobox.de/en/knowledge/412-short-circuit-current)
- Mitsubishi Lossnay Q&A on "short circulation" (outdoor grilles): keep intake and exhaust grilles as far apart as possible; an exposed location with free wind reduces short circulation; a diagram references ~3× duct diameter spacing (context unclear in extract). This is for outdoor terminals, not indoor grilles. — [Mitsubishi Lossnay Series manual, Q&A 13 (manualslib)](https://manualslib.com/manual/1571205/Mitsubishi-Electric-Lossnay-Series.html?page=104)
- **[RoT, forum]** UK MVHR practitioners: put bedroom supply valves in the corner farthest from the door; a supply near the door "will just end up being sucked out through the gap under the door"; if a supply must be near the door, use a Coanda-type valve mounted near the ceiling; put bathroom extract above the shower/bath, away from doors and windows; doors are undercut "to enable air flows from the supply bedrooms to the extract en suites." — [BuildHub MVHR threads, e.g. 26481](https://forum.buildhub.org.uk/topic/26481-mvhr-initial-design-critique), [22960](https://forum.buildhub.org.uk/topic/22960-mvhr-design/) (exact thread-to-quote attribution not verified)
- A Cardiff University thesis simulated how shrinking the distance between the supply vent and the door undercut increases short-circuiting (confirms the mechanism; it is a study, not a design rule). — [Cardiff ORCA 172233](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/172233)
- NHBC Standards (UK) only require MVHR to perform satisfactorily and be installed per design and manufacturer instructions; no geometric rule. — [NHBC 8.4.1.3](https://nhbc-standards.co.uk/8-services/8-4-ventilation/8-4-1-mechanical-ventilation-and-heat-recovery-mvhr/8-4-1-3-system-design/)

**Sidewall grille height, throw and drafts**
- Catalog throw for residential sidewall registers is defined as distance to a 50 fpm (≈0.25 m/s) terminal velocity; some products are rated at 75 fpm (≈0.38 m/s), so check the rating basis. — [Hart & Cooley catalog sheet](https://resource.ecmdi.com/is/content/Watscocom/Gemaire/hart-cooley_article_en_ss.pdf); [Metal-Fab sheet](https://resource.gemaire.com/is/content/Watscocom/Gemaire/metal-fab_mffr212b_article_1383140757909_en_ss.pdf?fmt=pdf) (exact catalog per quote not pinned in extract)
- Cataloged sidewall throw data assume the grille is "mounted 9" (229 mm) below the ceiling and benefiting from the ceiling" (Coanda); data are isothermal; "typical cold supply air (20 °F ΔT) reduces horizontal throw by approximately 30%", warm air increases it by about the same. — same catalog sheets as above
- Trox: a wall grille placed close to the ceiling achieves a greater throw than a comparable free jet because of the ceiling (Coanda) effect. — [TROX X-Grille](https://www.trox.de/en/wall-sill-and-duct-installation/x-grille-modular-487aed9d8456eb97)
- Colman (UK) defines sidewall grille throw as the distance from grille face to the opposite wall, with a down-wall velocity of about 0.4 m/s at the minimum throw value; test grille 1.25 m long mounted 0.2 m below the ceiling. — [Colman G-Series PDS](https://www.specifiedby.com/colman-air-distribution/g-series-supply-grille/Colman-Moducel_G-Series-Supply-Grille_Technical_PDS.pdf)
- The often-repeated **[RoT]** "throw should be about 75% of room length" could not be traced to ACCA Manual T or ASHRAE in this session (search found no source).

### Inferences
- For the 7 m × 4.3 m living room, the east-wall high sidewall grille should be mounted with its top within ~200–250 mm of the ceiling so the jet attaches to the ceiling, and selected so its catalog throw (to 0.25 m/s) at the actual airflow is roughly 5–7 m. At the far (west) wall the jet will then turn down at low velocity. Pick a double-deflection grille so the blades can spread the jet slightly across the 4.3 m width and tilt it a few degrees up.
- Airflow matters more than grille size: ERV supply to one room is often only tens of m³/h. A large grille at that flow gives a low face velocity and a short throw. To reach 7 m, use a smaller-faced grille, a slot/linear diffuser, or a long-throw nozzle, sharing a grille with the dehumidifier supply where possible. Check noise at the higher face velocity.
- In Taiwan's cooling season, both supply streams are likely warmer than the AC-cooled room. Enthalpy-recovered outdoor air is only partly cooled, and a compressor dehumidifier discharges warm, dry air. Warm jets cling to the ceiling, which helps the 7 m throw (+~30% per catalogs) but makes it harder for the air to reach the occupied zone. Room mixing will depend partly on the split AC's own jet.
- Keeping jets off people: if the jet is attached to the ceiling and the terminal velocity is reached near the far wall, occupants under the jet see little air speed. Don't aim the grille downward toward the sofa or bed, and don't place a supply grille where it discharges horizontally at head height.
- With no numeric standard, a defensible practice is: supply and the room's exit (door/transfer grille/return) at opposite ends or diagonals of the room. Never put a supply within about 1 m of the door or transfer opening it drains through **[RoT, inferred from forum and thesis findings]**.

### Gaps
- No manufacturer (Lossnay, Panasonic, Daikin, Zehnder) indoor minimum supply–return spacing figure was found. Full Zehnder/Brink design guides and the Lossnay technical manual could not be fetched.
- ACCA Manual T / ASHRAE Fundamentals (room air distribution chapter) text on throw-to-room-length ratios, the "return location has little effect on room air motion" principle and occupied-zone velocity limits (ASHRAE 55 / ISO 7730 draft limits, usually cited around 0.2–0.25 m/s) was not retrieved. Treat those as unverified here.
- No Taiwanese contractor article with specific indoor grille distances (公尺) was found. Taiwan sources only give the qualitative "keep apart to avoid short-circuit" rule.

## Q2. Transfer paths: door undercuts and transfer grille free area per m³/h so a supply-only room can return air

### Takeaway
UK Approved Document F uses a 10 mm undercut (≈7,600 mm² = 76 cm² for a 760 mm door) as the default transfer path for ventilation-rate flows. BSC sizes transfer paths so a closed bedroom stays at ≤3 Pa, using Q(cfm) = 1.07·A(in²)·√ΔP(Pa). In metric that works out to about **2 cm² of free area per m³/h at 3 Pa** (≈7 cm² per L/s) and **about 3–3.5 cm² per m³/h at 1 Pa**. Undercuts are fine for ERV-only flows (tens of m³/h), but a recirculating ducted dehumidifier (hundreds of m³/h) into a closed bedroom needs a transfer grille, jump duct or ducted return.

### Cited Findings
- UK ADF (2021) Vol 1 para 1.25, as quoted in Zehnder's compliance checklist: internal doors need an undercut for air transfer of "10 mm above the floor finish or 20 mm above the floor surface" (the latter if the finish is not yet fitted). — [Zehnder ADF checklist (existing dwellings)](https://www.zehnder.co.uk/fileadmin/user_upload/UK/adf-2010-2021-edition---checklist-for-ventilation-in-existing-dwellings__1_.pdf)
- UK trade press: "replacement doors should have a minimum air gap of 7,600 mm²", which for a typical 760 mm door is a 10 mm (3/8") undercut above the floor finish. — [PBC Today](https://pbctoday.co.uk/news/?p=93189)
- Building Science Corporation RR-0005 (transfer grille sizing): based on a square-edged orifice, Q = 1.07 × A × √ΔP (Q in cfm, A = free area in in², ΔP in Pa). The design goal is to keep a closed bedroom from pressurizing above 3 Pa. Base case: 32" door with a 0.5" undercut. In suites, total the supply flows and size the transfer path for the total. Free area is adjusted for extra friction (factors 0.6 for high/low wall transfer, 0.7 for through-wall transfer and jump ducts, per extract; check exact use in the original). — [BSC RR-0005](https://buildingscience.com/documents/reports/rr-0005-transfer-grille-sizing/view); [GBA-hosted PDF "Jump Duct and Transfer Grille Sizing"](https://images.greenbuildingadvisor.com/app/uploads/2019/10/24093847/Jump-Duct-and-Transfer-Grille-Sizing.pdf)
- BSC Info-804 ("What's wrong with this practice? Undercutting doors"): undercutting seldom gives adequate pressure relief for forced-air return flows. Prefer through-wall transfer grilles or jump ducts, which keep air inside the conditioned space. — [BSC Info-804](https://www.buildingscience.com/documents/information-sheets/information-sheet-wrong-undercutting-doors)
- For forced-air HVAC return flows, one building-science source says the undercut "would need to be 1.5 inches or greater" in most applications (≈38 mm). Contrasting field view: an energy rater reports "a half inch door undercut works just fine in most of the homes he tests." Air leaking around the jambs and head can equal the undercut flow, so undercut-only calculations underestimate real transfer. — [GBA "When are door undercuts sufficient for return air"](https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/article/when-are-door-undercuts-sufficient-for-return-air); [ACCA blog version](https://hvac-blog.acca.org/?p=17970); [BASC "Undercut doors"](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/undercut-doors) (search extract did not pin which of these gave the 1.5" figure)
- German door maker Garant gives undercut airflow "gemäß DIN 1946-6" as a factor × door width (cm) × undercut height (cm), with factors 0.23 / 0.32 / 0.40 at 0.5 / 1.0 / 1.5 Pa. Example: 0.32 × 85 cm × 0.7 cm = 19 m³/h at 1 Pa. — [Garant Türenhandbuch](https://tuerenhandbuch.garant.de/typo3conf/ext/cdt_cmos2/Resources/Public/cmos2/php/get.php?id=5488)
- **[RoT, forum]** German MVHR forums: 8–12 mm undercut above finished floor; 80–120 cm² or 150 cm² per door "per DIN 1946-6" (unverified, contradictory); 50–100 cm² for 20–50 m³/h at 1–2 Pa. — [bau.de forum 10436](https://bau.de/forum/lueftung/10436.php); [bau.net 10011](https://bau.net/forum/lueftung/10011.php)
- **[RoT]** "1 in² free area per cfm" for return/transfer grilles (more conservative than BSC at 3 Pa; ≈3.8 cm² per m³/h). — [aircondlounge return grille guide](https://aircondlounge.com/return-air-grille-sizing-guide-cfm-chart-calculation/)
- Grille "free area" is defined differently by different manufacturers. Hart & Cooley distinguishes geometric free area from a lab-measured effective-area factor (Ak). — [aircondlounge](https://aircondlounge.com/return-air-grille-sizing-guide-cfm-chart-calculation/) (secondary)

### Inferences (calculations derived from the cited equations)
- Metric form of the BSC equation (1 cfm = 1.699 m³/h; 1 in² = 6.45 cm²): **Q[m³/h] ≈ 0.28 × A[cm²] × √ΔP[Pa]**. The DIN/Garant factor gives ≈0.32 × A × √ΔP, so the two agree within ~15% (implied discharge coefficient ≈0.6).
  - Required free area ≈ **3.1–3.6 cm² per m³/h at 1 Pa** (11–13 cm² per L/s); **1.8–2.1 cm² per m³/h at 3 Pa** (6.5–7.4 cm² per L/s).
- Undercut capacity for an 80 cm door (gap only, excluding jamb leakage):
  - 10 mm (80 cm²) ≈ 22–26 m³/h at 1 Pa, ≈39–44 m³/h at 3 Pa.
  - 20 mm (160 cm²) ≈ 45–51 m³/h at 1 Pa, ≈78–89 m³/h at 3 Pa.
  - 25 mm (200 cm²) ≈ 56–64 m³/h at 1 Pa, ≈97–110 m³/h at 3 Pa.
- Master bedroom, door closed:
  - ERV-only supply of ~30–60 m³/h: a 10–20 mm undercut keeps ΔP at or below ~3 Pa.
  - ERV plus ducted-dehumidifier supply of, say, 150–250 m³/h: needs ~300–500 cm² free area at 3 Pa. That is a 40×20 cm to 60×20 cm class transfer grille (gross area larger, since free area is often 50–70% of gross), an acoustic jump duct, or a ducted return from the bedroom back to the unit. The dehumidifier airflow figures are illustrative; get the real airflow from the chosen model's datasheet.
- Passive House practice uses ~1 Pa-class overflow losses (energy-driven). BSC uses ≤3 Pa (comfort/infiltration-driven). Use 3 Pa as an upper bound and ~1–2 Pa as a target where an acoustic transfer grille fits.
- Place the bedroom's transfer opening (high-level transfer grille, or the door undercut) at the opposite end of the room from the supply above the wardrobe, unless the wardrobe is beside the door. Otherwise the supply short-circuits straight out (see Q1).

### Gaps
- Full text of ADF 2021 para 1.25 and the PHI/Passipedia overflow-pressure criterion (often quoted as ≤1 Pa) could not be fetched and confirmed in this session.
- Acoustic (privacy) performance of transfer grilles vs undercuts was not researched.

## Q3. Bathroom/laundry exhaust fans with an ERV: balance, depressurisation, positive vs negative pressure effects (odour, moisture, doors), and whether the ERV should extract from the bathroom in Taiwan

### Takeaway
In hot-humid climates, sustained depressurisation (exhaust > supply) pulls humid outdoor air through the envelope and into wall cavities. Neutral-to-slightly-positive pressure is preferred, with intermittent spot exhaust in wet rooms. Mitsubishi's whole-house Lossnay diagrams do show WC and shower rooms as extract points. However, Mitsubishi's own toilet-specific Lossnay switched to a sensible-only, high-airtight element to stop water-soluble odour (ammonia) migrating with moisture, and Taiwan's Delta keeps the unit away from WCs and kitchens. For Taiwan, keeping the bathroom and laundry on their own direct-to-outside fans and extracting the ERV from the hall/transfer zone is the lower-risk choice. Accept that each spot fan run briefly depressurises 2F.

### Cited Findings
**Pressure regimes in humid climates**
- US DOE: "In climates with warm humid summers, depressurization can draw moist air into building wall cavities, where it may condense and cause moisture damage." Balanced systems suit all climates. Supply systems let incoming air be filtered and dehumidified. — [Energy.gov Energy Saver: Whole-House Ventilation](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/articles/whole-house-ventilation)
- Building America: central-fan-integrated supply "creates slight positive pressures in the house, which helps reduce infiltration of unfiltered, humid air in hot, humid climates". "Semi-balanced" supply plus timed exhaust is a low-cost alternative to an H/ERV. Positive pressure is the wrong choice in cold climates (it pushes moist indoor air into walls). — [DOE Building America: Low-Cost Ventilation](https://www.energy.gov/eere/buildings/articles/low-cost-ventilation-production-housing-building-america-top-innovation)
- BSC BSI-012, "Balancing Act – Exhaust-Only Ventilation Does Not Work" (in hot/humid regions). BSC also notes part-load humidity in hot-humid climates "cannot be controlled without supplemental dehumidification." — [BSC BSI-012](https://www.buildingscience.com/documents/building-science-insights-newsletters/bsi-012-balancing-act-exhaust-only-ventilation-does)
- Humid-climate ventilation options: balanced ventilation without recovery or dehumidification dumps humid air indoors. Supply-only with humidistat control is another option. — [GBA "6 ways to ventilate homes in humid climates"](https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/article/6-ways-to-ventilate-homes-in-humid-climates); [Energy Vanguard](https://energyvanguard.com/blog/6-options-for-ventilating-homes-in-humid-climates)
- Building America (CARB, existing homes): exhaust-only gives no control over where make-up air enters and "often does not dilute contaminants equally throughout a home." — [Building America: Selecting Ventilation Systems for Existing Homes](https://www.energy.gov/eere/buildings/articles/building-america-technology-solutions-new-and-existing-homes-selecting)

**ERV and bathroom extract**
- Mitsubishi whole-house Lossnay catalogues show toilets and shower rooms as exhaust points and describe one unit ventilating "from living room and bedrooms to the bathroom". Their paper core exchanges heat and moisture, with supply and exhaust passages kept separate (no mixing). — [Mitsubishi Lossnay 2017 brochure (AU)](https://www.mitsubishielectric.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/MIT-Lossnay-2017-32pp_Artwork_Digital-Singles.pdf); [Lossnay construction page (manualslib)](https://Manualslib.com/manual/1571205/Mitsubishi-Electric-Lossnay-Series.html?page=25)
- Mitsubishi technical report: the toilet-local Lossnay LKY-50RX was designed to stop toilet odour migrating from the exhaust path to the supply path, using "a high-airtight **sensible** heat exchange element so that ammonia dissolved in the humidity component does not physically transfer." This implies that a total-heat (enthalpy) element can carry water-soluble odourants along with moisture. — [Mitsubishi Electric Giho 2016](https://www.giho.mitsubishielectric.co.jp/giho/pdf/2016/1610113.pdf) (attribution to this PDF inferred from search listing)
- Mitsubishi (JP) duct-Lossnay page: standard total-heat type "梅雨時の除湿には使えません" (cannot be used for dehumidification in the rainy season). — [Mitsubishi duct Lossnay](https://www.mitsubishielectric.co.jp/ldm/ventilationfan/ductlossnay/)
- Delta (Taiwan): avoid installing the ERV in high-temperature/high-humidity locations such as kitchens and toilets. — [Delta FAQ 2020](https://www.deltaww.com/zh-tw/FAQ/2020)
- North American ERV manufacturer installation document (EV70–EV300 series): when the ERV provides bathroom exhaust, EV70 → 1 bathroom, EV130 → ≤2, EV200 → ≤4, EV300 → ≤6, with at least 50 cfm (≈85 m³/h) of exhaust per moisture-producing bathroom. — [ERV spec/installation doc in Portland ME permit file](https://parcelsfolder.portlandmaine.gov/Files/259/259%20%20C021/Building%20Permits/2016-00888%20HVAC/2.%20Documents/Air%20handler%20specs.pdf) (brand not identified in extract)
- **[RoT, forum]** Humid-climate practitioners: put the ERV pickup in the most-used bathroom and standard exhaust fans in the others. A shower needs about a 100 cfm pickup, so most ERVs can serve only about one bathroom. — [GBA Q&A "Venting bathrooms separate from an ERV in a humid climate"](https://greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/venting-bathrooms-separate-from-an-erv-in-a-humid-climate)
- Bathroom exhaust must discharge outdoors (not into the ceiling void). Timer or humidity-sensing fans are recommended (Taiwan consumer media, weak source). — [TVBS Health](https://health.tvbs.com.tw/life/333787)

**Doors / room pressure**
- BSC's ≤3 Pa bedroom criterion (Q2) is the cited threshold for closed-door pressurisation problems. Varying pressure across a door can hold it shut or push it open (patent literature on pressure-controlled doors). — [BSC RR-0005](https://buildingscience.com/documents/reports/rr-0005-transfer-grille-sizing/view); [US patent 11435098](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11435098)

### Inferences
- **Enthalpy-core physics in Taiwan's summer.** Moisture moves from the more humid stream to the drier one. Post-shower bathroom air (often near saturation) is more humid than even humid outdoor air, so an ERV extracting from the bathroom would hand part of that shower moisture, plus water-soluble odour, back to the supply air. The Mitsubishi LKY-50RX design choice supports this. That conflicts with the dehumidifier's job. Keep the bathroom and laundry on their own direct exhaust fans to the rear window, as planned.
- **Pressure when spot fans run.** A balanced ERV plus a running bath or laundry fan leaves 2F net negative by the fan's flow. Make-up air enters through envelope leaks and the stairwell from the other floors, which can carry humid outdoor air and stairwell or 1F odours. Mitigations:
  - Run spot fans only on timer or humidistat.
  - Close the bathroom door while the fan runs, so the fan draws mostly from the bathroom and the rest of 2F stays near neutral.
  - Optionally set the ERV a few percent supply-biased (slightly positive) if its fan speeds are independently adjustable. This follows the DOE/Building America humid-climate preference.
- **Odour containment.** Wet rooms should be the most negative zones (they are, whenever their fans run). Bedroom and living room should be neutral or slightly positive, so odour flows bedroom/living → hall → out, never hall/bath → bedroom.
- **Doors slamming or whistling.** These show up when a closed room exceeds a few Pa. Sizing transfer paths for ≤3 Pa (Q2) and avoiding large unbalanced exhaust (for example a kitchen hood or dryer on 2F) prevents them.

### Gaps
- No direct manufacturer statement was found ("do / don't use enthalpy ERV for bathroom exhaust in hot-humid climates"), and no measured odour/moisture carry-over rates for paper enthalpy cores. Panasonic and Daikin Taiwan installation manuals were not retrieved.
- No quantified source on door-slamming thresholds (force vs Pa) was found.

## Q4. Ducted dehumidifier air distribution: where to put its return, where to supply, using a storage room/corridor as a return plenum, and how it relates to the ERV and a wall-mounted split AC

### Takeaway
Manufacturer guidance (Ultra-Aire/Santa Fe) is a **dedicated return from a central, open area, never from a bathroom or kitchen**, with supply ducted to the rooms that need dry air (in homes with ductless AC, "to the rooms … that have the ductless" units). Practitioners send dehumidifier and ERV supply to bedrooms and living areas. If the two systems share supply ducts, use a backdraft damper on the dehumidifier branch, and keep the dehumidifier's own return, because its flow does not match the ERV's.

### Cited Findings
- Ultra-Aire 98H/120H: "Create a separate return for the Ultra-Aire … in a central area of the building. Duct the supply … to the air supply of the HVAC system with a backdraft damper." If the check damper is not in place, the HVAC fan must run when the dehumidifier runs. — [Ultra-Aire 98H/120H installations](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0235/2395/0639/files/Ultra-Aire-98H-120H-Installations.pdf)
- Ultra-Aire 70H recommended installation: "DO NOT locate return in a bathroom or kitchen"; the return should come from an open area of the first or second floor. — [Ultra-Aire 70H recommended installation](https://www.sylvane.com/cdn/shop/files/recommended-installation-ultra-aire-70h-dehumidifier.pdf)
- Alternative layout: dehumidifier supply into the HVAC return side, in which case the HVAC fan must run whenever the dehumidifier runs. — [Ultra-Aire 70H alternative installation](https://www.sylvane.com/cdn/shop/files/alternative-installation-ultra-aire-70h-dehumidifier.pdf)
- Santa Fe Ultra series (homes with ductless/mini-split AC): "The supply of the Ultra Series Dehumidifier should be ducted to the rooms in the home that have the ductless" system. — [Santa Fe Ultra series manual (manuals.online)](https://manuals.online/santa-fe/dehumidifier/ultra-series/user-manual/ugdk) (attribution per search extract)
- Ductless-home layout used by practitioners: dedicated return grille (for example in a hallway ceiling) feeding the dehumidifier, and a supply grille into a central area. Supply/return placement near a mini-split's sensor can make it under-dehumidify. — [GBA Q&A: Supply and return placement for dehumidifier with ductless A/C](https://greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/supply-and-return-placement-for-dehumidifier-with-ductless-a-c); [Energy Vanguard "4 ways to duct a dehumidifier"](https://energyvanguard.com/blog/4-ways-to-duct-a-dehumidifier)
- GBA (dehumidifier + ERV + forced air):
  - "The supply air from the dehumidifier and ERV should go to the bedrooms," while the dehumidifier's dedicated return pulls "from a large central portion of the home."
  - A backdraft damper is needed "on the dehumidifier supply duct prior to connection with the ERV," otherwise ERV supply air exits via the dehumidifier return.
  - The dehumidifier needs its own return because "the flow from the dehumidifier will not match the flow of the ERV supply."
  - "The ERV will go slightly unbalanced and will depressurize the house when the dehumidifier is running" if the ERV was balanced with the dehumidifier off.
  — [GBA "Ductwork for ERVs, dehumidifiers and forced-air heating systems"](https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/article/ductwork-ervs-dehumidifiers-forced-air-heating-systems)
- Florida Building Commission / FSEC study tested dehumidifier (DHU) locations: not ducted, ducted directly from/to a room, from/to the central return, and from/to the central supply. — [FSEC DHU Location Final Report 2018](https://www.floridabuilding.org/fbc/commission/FBC_0618/Energy_TAC/DHU-Location-Final-Report-06012018.pdf)
- Taiwan consumer/contractor guidance: whole-house ceiling-concealed dehumidifiers connect by duct to each room's supply and return. Plan the drain, the power outlet near the unit, and filter and maintenance access (filter usually at the access hatch). Whole-house units are marketed for about 25 ping (≈83 m²), single-zone units for 8–10 ping. No grille-position rules given. — [PRO360 舊屋翻新除濕機指南](https://www.pro360.com.tw/guide/old_house_reno_dehumidifier)
- Taiwan residential ERV example: LG LZ-H015GBA6 uses 125 mm duct collars (4 ports) and lists external static pressure by fan speed (useful as a scale of residential ERV duct sizes). — [LG Taiwan ERV](https://www.lg.com/tw/residential-erv-ventilation/residential-erv-ventilation/lz-h015gba6/)
- Mitsubishi: the ERV (total heat exchanger) is not a dehumidifier in the rainy season. — [Mitsubishi duct Lossnay](https://www.mitsubishielectric.co.jp/ldm/ventilationfan/ductlossnay/)

### Inferences
- **"Pull the wettest air" vs "central return."** Manufacturers deliberately keep the return out of baths and kitchens (high-moisture, lint/grease, odour spikes, and the risk of pulling from a room that is also being exhausted). A central hall return that drains both living room and bedroom is the documented pattern. A dehumidifier dries the whole well-mixed volume, so return location matters less than circulation. Indoor clothes drying in the laundry is the exception: if the user wants the dehumidifier to handle it, keep the laundry door open with the laundry fan off, so moisture migrates to the hall return. Running the laundry fan instead just exhausts the moisture (and conditioned air) outdoors. Pick one strategy per drying session.
- **Storage room (or corridor) as return plenum.** This works if:
  1. The plenum is sealed except for the intended louvre. Under-stair spaces are often open to the stairwell or 1F/3F voids, which would make the return pull air from other floors.
  2. The louvre's free area is sized for the dehumidifier's full airflow plus the ERV extract at roughly 2 m/s face velocity or less (quiet).
  3. No combustion appliance or chemicals are stored there.
  4. The ERV's extract and the dehumidifier's return can share this plenum, but the dehumidifier's supply duct and the ERV supply must be fully ducted and sealed through it. Any supply leak into the plenum is an indoor short-circuit.
  Note: US code restricts return air from closets (see Gaps).
- **Dehumidifier vs ERV.** Duct the dehumidifier to deliver dry air to the bedroom and living room, either through its own grilles or teed into the ERV supply with a backdraft damper on the dehumidifier branch. Keep its own dedicated return. If the ERV's extract shares the plenum with the dehumidifier return, the ERV stays balanced overall (the dehumidifier is pure recirculation). Room-level pressures then depend on transfer paths (Q2).
- **Dehumidifier vs split AC.** Per Santa Fe, deliver dry air into the rooms the split serves. The 7 m throw from the east wall reaches the west wall where the split hangs. Its intake (on top of the indoor unit) will entrain the arriving jet and redistribute it, which helps mixing. The jet should arrive at terminal velocity, not blow straight into the intake at close range. Warm dehumidifier discharge reaching the split's return thermistor would make the AC run harder or longer, and the split's coil also removes latent load, so the two machines can work against each other. Run the AC in cooling with the dehumidifier on humidistat, and avoid AC "dry" mode overlapping.
- The bedroom supply above the wardrobe should throw toward the far side of the room from the bedroom's exit (door/transfer grille), and not down onto the bed.

### Gaps
- US IRC M1602.2 (return air may not be taken from closets, bathrooms, kitchens and similar spaces, with exceptions) was not verified in this session. Taiwan has no equivalent residential rule found.
- No Taiwanese manufacturer (e.g. Panasonic TW, 日立, 大金) installation manual for ceiling-concealed dehumidifiers with explicit return/supply placement rules was retrieved.
- Typical airflow (m³/h) of Taiwanese ducted dehumidifiers was not sourced; the bedroom transfer-area estimate in Q2 uses illustrative flows.

## Q5. Application to the stated 2F layout (synthesis of the above)

### Takeaway
The proposed scheme is consistent with documented practice: supply to living room and bedroom, a central hall/storage return, and wet rooms on their own exhaust. The main risks are:
1. Bedroom pressurisation when its door is closed and dehumidifier air is added. This needs a sized transfer path or ducted return, not just an undercut.
2. Throw: the living-room sidewall grille must actually reach ~7 m at the real (possibly small) airflow.
3. Leak paths around the under-stair plenum.
4. The ERV going unbalanced if the dehumidifier is teed into its ducts without a damper.

### Cited Findings
- Central return, not bath/kitchen; supply to rooms served by ductless AC. — [Ultra-Aire 70H](https://www.sylvane.com/cdn/shop/files/recommended-installation-ultra-aire-70h-dehumidifier.pdf); [Santa Fe Ultra manual](https://manuals.online/santa-fe/dehumidifier/ultra-series/user-manual/ugdk)
- Supply zone (living/bed) → transfer zone (corridor) → extract zone (wet rooms). — [Detail Passive House Design](https://issuu.com/detail-magazine/docs/bk_passive_house_design/12)
- Bedroom ≤3 Pa via sized transfer path; Q = 1.07·A·√ΔP (cfm, in², Pa). — [BSC RR-0005](https://buildingscience.com/documents/reports/rr-0005-transfer-grille-sizing/view)
- Sidewall grille near ceiling (~200–230 mm), throw to 0.25 m/s, ±30% for non-isothermal air. — [Hart & Cooley](https://resource.ecmdi.com/is/content/Watscocom/Gemaire/hart-cooley_article_en_ss.pdf); [Colman](https://www.specifiedby.com/colman-air-distribution/g-series-supply-grille/Colman-Moducel_G-Series-Supply-Grille_Technical_PDS.pdf)
- Backdraft damper and a separate return when dehumidifier and ERV share supply ducts; the ERV depressurises if it was balanced without the dehumidifier. — [GBA](https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/article/ductwork-ervs-dehumidifiers-forced-air-heating-systems)
- Slight positive / balanced pressure preferred in humid climates. — [Energy.gov](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/articles/whole-house-ventilation)

### Inferences (checklist for the report writer)
1. **Living room.** High sidewall grille on the east wall, top edge ≤250 mm below the ceiling. Choose it from the catalog throw table for ~5–7 m to 0.25 m/s at the actual combined ERV + dehumidifier flow, blades set slightly up and spread. Air returns along the lower room toward the hall: that is the sweep, so no separate living-room return is needed if the living room opens fully to the hall.
2. **Short-circuit check.** The hall louvre on the storage door must not sit right beside or under the living-room or bedroom supply grilles on the same wall face. If both are on the storage-room walls, keep the supply high and the return low, and keep at least ~1–2 m of wall between them **[RoT]**, or put the louvre on a face that sees neither supply directly.
3. **Bedroom.** Supply above the wardrobe, aimed away from the bed and toward the far side from the door. Transfer path sized for the total bedroom supply at ≤3 Pa, about 2 cm² free area per m³/h. A 10–20 mm undercut covers ERV-only flows of about 40–90 m³/h. With dehumidifier flow add an acoustic transfer grille or jump duct (≈300–500 cm² free for 150–250 m³/h), or a ducted bedroom return.
4. **Wet rooms.** Bathroom and laundry keep their own exhaust to the rear window on timer or humidistat. Don't connect them to the enthalpy ERV (moisture and odour carry-over; Delta and Mitsubishi cautions). Expect short depressurisation of 2F when they run. Keep the ERV balanced or slightly supply-biased.
5. **Storage-room plenum.** Seal the under-stair cavity from stairwell and other floors, seal all supply ducts and boots passing through it, size the louvre free area for the total return flow (dehumidifier + ERV extract) at a low face velocity, and provide filter access.
6. **ERV–dehumidifier coupling.** Separate supply grilles, or a shared duct with a backdraft damper on the dehumidifier branch. The dehumidifier keeps its own return. Re-check ERV balance with the dehumidifier on and off.
7. **Split AC.** Let the sidewall jet arrive at the west wall at terminal velocity. Don't aim dehumidifier discharge directly at the split's intake or thermistor at short range. Coordinate control (AC cooling with the dehumidifier on humidistat; avoid simultaneous AC "dry" mode).

### Gaps
- Actual airflow ratings of the chosen ERV and dehumidifier (and the split's position relative to the living room door) were not provided, so grille sizes and throw selections above are ranges, not final selections.
- Whether the under-stair storage room is open to the stairwell and the 1F/3F voids is unknown and decisive for using it as a plenum.
